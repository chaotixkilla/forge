# Lead with the takeaway

The reader decides whether to keep reading in the first sentence, and they read to find out what it means for them — not to follow the writer's path to the conclusion. An artifact that opens with background, chronology, or setup makes every reader do the work of finding the point, and the busy ones simply don't: they skim, miss the ask, and the artifact fails silently. This rule is not the slogan "put the point first" — the hard part is *finding* the point and telling it apart from the material that feels like it must come first. It is applied in [frame-the-message](../phases/01-frame-the-message.md) (fixing the takeaway) and [draft-the-content](../phases/04-draft-the-content.md) (opening with it).

## Find the takeaway: the deletion test

The takeaway is the **conclusion** — the thing everything else in the artifact is evidence *for*. To find it, ask of each candidate sentence: *if the reader read only this, could they act correctly?* The sentence that passes is the takeaway; the sentences that only make it believable are setup.

The discriminator between takeaway and setup, stated as a test:
- **Setup** is what the reader needs to *believe or contextualize* the takeaway — the background, the chronology, the how-we-got-here. Cut a piece of setup and the reader still knows what to do; they just know it with less supporting context.
- **Takeaway** is what the reader *acts on* — the decision, the result, the ask. Cut it and the reader is left with context and no conclusion: they know the situation but not what it means or what to do.

Run the test by elimination: draft the candidate opening, delete it, and read what remains. If the reader can still act, you deleted setup — the real takeaway is still below, keep going. If the reader is now stranded, you deleted the takeaway — that is what belongs first.

## The trap: the chronology reflex

The strongest pull toward burying the point is narrative order — writers recount events in the sequence they happened ("we investigated X, then found Y, which led us to Z") because that is how they *lived* it. But the reader wants Z first. Invert it: state the conclusion, then let the evidence and the path follow for whoever wants to verify it. The chronology is setup; the outcome is the takeaway. This is the same move as the inverted pyramid — most important first, supporting detail after, so a reader who stops anywhere still has the most important thing. `(basis: inverted-pyramid / BLUF — bottom-line-up-front; a well-corroborated principle across journalism, plain-language, and professional-writing style guides.)`

## Anchors

- *Buried (the defect):* "Over the past two weeks we evaluated three caching strategies. We benchmarked LRU, LFU, and a hybrid. After analysis, we're recommending the hybrid." → The reader wades through method to reach the recommendation.
- *Led (the fix):* "**Recommendation: adopt the hybrid cache.** We evaluated LRU, LFU, and hybrid over two weeks; the hybrid won on tail latency — details below." → The decision is the first thing read; the method is there for whoever wants it.

## When leading with the takeaway is genuinely wrong

Two narrow cases, both bounded: a piece written specifically to *teach reasoning* (a tutorial, a mentoring walkthrough) may build toward the conclusion so the learner follows the derivation — but that is the learning-mode overlay ([meet-the-learner-where-they-are](meet-the-learner-where-they-are.md)) making a deliberate call, not a license to bury; and hard news delivered to a person may need a sentence of framing before the conclusion for humane reasons — one sentence, not a paragraph of setup. Outside these, the takeaway leads.
