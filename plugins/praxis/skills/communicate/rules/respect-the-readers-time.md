# Respect the reader's time

Every sentence in an artifact is read by everyone it reaches, so a wasted sentence is a tax multiplied by the audience. Hedging, throat-clearing, and redundancy feel harmless to the writer — they cost nothing to leave in — but they push the point further down, train readers that this source pads, and make a busy reader stop before the part that mattered. This rule is the word-level cut, and it sits on a clean seam with [right-size-the-detail](right-size-the-detail.md): that rule decides *which facts* the artifact carries, this one decides *how tightly each surviving fact is written*. It is applied in [tighten-and-verify](../phases/06-tighten-and-verify.md).

## What to cut

- **Throat-clearing** — the runway before the point: "I just wanted to reach out to say," "It's worth noting that," "As you may know." Delete it; start at the content.
- **Hedging that adds no information** — "sort of," "I think maybe," "it seems like it might possibly." Where genuine uncertainty is a fact the reader needs, state it precisely ("I'm ~70% on this"); where it's just softening, cut it. Real uncertainty is content; reflexive hedging is filler.
- **Redundancy** — the same point made twice, the summary that repeats the section it summarizes, the qualifier that restates the previous clause.
- **Nominalizations and filler verbs** — "make a decision" → "decide," "provide assistance" → "help," "in order to" → "to." The shorter form carries the same meaning with less to read.

## The test: every word tells

The bar, applied sentence by sentence: **can this sentence be deleted without losing a fact the reader needs?** If yes, it was filler — cut it. Can a phrase within it be shortened without losing meaning? Then shorten it. This is the same "omit needless words" discipline that closes the altitude stopping test — here it runs at the word and sentence level over the details that already survived the need-to-know cut, so the two rules compose rather than overlap. `(basis: Strunk & White "omit needless words"; plain-language concision guidance — corroborated across style authorities as the sentence-level economy bar.)`

## Concision is not terseness

Cutting words serves the reader; it does not mean stripping the artifact of the context or warmth a human reader needs. A message that's so compressed it reads as curt, or that drops the one framing sentence that makes hard news land humanely, has over-cut — that framing sentence *is* content ([calibrate-tone-to-context](calibrate-tone-to-context.md)). The target is every sentence earning its place, not the fewest possible sentences; delete what doesn't serve the reader, keep what does, including what serves them as a person.
