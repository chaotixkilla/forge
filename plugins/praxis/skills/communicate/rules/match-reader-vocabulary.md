# Match reader vocabulary

Jargon is a tool for compression among people who share it and a wall for everyone else. The same acronym that lets two peers skip a paragraph forces a newcomer to stop and look it up, and signals to an external reader that this wasn't written for them. Writers reach for their own vocabulary by default — it's what's loaded — so this rule makes vocabulary a deliberate choice keyed to the tier, not a reflex. It is applied in [draft-the-content](../phases/04-draft-the-content.md) and checked against the tier in [tighten-and-verify](../phases/05-tighten-and-verify.md).

## The three moves, keyed to the tier

Every domain term, acronym, and internal name gets one of three treatments, and which one is set by the reader's proximity (Axis A of [audience-tiers](audience-tiers.md)):

- **Use bare** — the reader already holds the term (peer). Defining it wastes their time and reads as condescension.
- **Define on first use** — the reader knows the domain but not this specific term (newcomer, or a peer meeting a term from an adjacent area). A parenthetical or a clause, once, then use it freely.
- **Drop and rephrase** — the reader doesn't share the domain and doesn't need the term to act (exec, external). Replace it with the plain-language thing it denotes; don't define jargon the reader will never use again, just say the thing.

The discriminator between *define* and *drop*: will the reader **use this term again** — in this artifact, or in the work it leads to? If yes, define it (they need the handle). If no, drop it (the definition is overhead for a word they'll never reuse). An exec reading a one-time decision record doesn't need "idempotent" defined; they need "safe to retry."

## Don't display vocabulary

The failure this prevents is jargon used to *signal expertise* rather than to communicate — the term that's technically correct but chosen because it sounds precise, where a plain word would land for more readers. The test: if a simpler word carries the same meaning for *this* reader, the jargon is display, not compression — cut it. `(basis: plain-language guidance and the Microsoft/Google style principle "avoid jargon; write for your reader" — jargon earns its place only by compressing for a reader who shares it, never by demonstrating the writer's command of it.)`
