# Weight by source strength

Every claim deep-research keeps is weighed by how strong the source behind it is, and that weight is what lets verification and synthesis do their jobs: which claims to trust, which to corroborate, which to treat as leads. If the scale is undefined, each run invents its own ladder — one treats a vendor blog as authoritative, another as marketing — and the same evidence yields two different answers. Source strength is a property of *one source*; it is not the same as how sure the claim is (that is the [claim-confidence-scale](claim-confidence-scale.md), which combines strength with corroboration). Weigh by the source's *basis* — its proximity to the origin, its method, its author's expertise, its independence, its currency — never by how confidently or fluently it asserts. This rule is cited from [verify-claims](../phases/04-verify-claims.md) and [synthesize](../phases/05-synthesize.md).

## The five tiers

`(basis: the common core of established evidence-evaluation frameworks — the axes that recur across GRADE (study design/methodological basis), the Oxford CEBM 2011 Levels of Evidence, the primary/secondary/tertiary source taxonomy (proximity to origin), SIFT and CRAAP (authority, purpose/independence, currency), Wikipedia's WP:RS/WP:INDEPENDENT (reputation, independence), and ICD 203 / the Admiralty code (source reliability judged separately from the claim). These five axes — proximity to origin, methodological basis, expertise, independence, currency — are the derived common core.)`

`(basis: ratified by the maintainer, 2026-07-13. The exact tier boundaries and the two end anchors below are the ratified house standard, resting on the derived common-core axes above — source strength, unlike clinical evidence, has no single external authority that fixes a general-purpose web ladder, so the rung lines and anchors are the maintainer's ratified call.)`

- **primary-authoritative** — the original, accountable source of the claim, produced with methodological rigor and domain expertise, and with no vested interest that would shape it: an official standard or specification from the body that defines it, a peer-reviewed primary study, primary data, or a maintainer's authoritative reference for their own system.
  - *Anchor (top):* the language/protocol specification, or the maintainer's official API reference, for a claim about what that system is *defined* to do.
- **vetted-secondary** — an expert synthesis or interpretation of primary sources carrying editorial oversight or peer review: a systematic review or meta-analysis, a scholarly book from an academic press, an authoritative reference work, or established journalism that cites and traces to primaries.
- **expert-attributed** — an identified domain expert's first-hand analysis or a documented practitioner report, *without* peer review or editorial vetting: a named expert's technical write-up, a conference talk, a well-evidenced engineering blog by someone with standing on the topic.
- **community-anecdotal** — unvetted community reports and opinion, with variable or unverifiable expertise and no editorial oversight: forum posts, Q&A answers, most blogs, aggregator content.
- **unattributable-or-conflicted** — content with no identifiable accountable author, **or** a vested interest that is the claim's primary warrant with nothing a reader could cross-check the claim against (promotional/marketing copy, a bare figure with no benchmark, comparator, or method named, an SEO content farm), **or** a claim reachable only through a circular echo chain with no traceable origin.
  - *Anchor (bottom):* an anonymous or marketing page asserting a benchmark result with no method, no author, and no primary anyone can reach.

## The adjacent-tier discriminators

Assign by walking down until a tier fits; the boundary tests are what stop a source sliding between two rungs:

- **primary-authoritative vs vetted-secondary** — is this the *origin* of the claim, or an expert's *synthesis* of others' primaries? Both carry rigor and vetting; the split is **proximity to origin** ([prefer-primary-sources](prefer-primary-sources.md)).
- **vetted-secondary vs expert-attributed** — does it carry editorial oversight or peer review, or is it one expert's unvetted word? The split is **vetting**.
- **expert-attributed vs community-anecdotal** — is the author an identifiable domain expert with standing on *this* topic, or unverifiable/general community voice? The split is **attributable expertise**.
- **community-anecdotal vs unattributable-or-conflicted** — is there an accountable author with no disqualifying conflict, or no accountable author / a vested interest / a broken chain? The split is **accountability + independence**.

When two tiers seem to fit, the higher wins only if you can name the property that justifies it (the primary it traces to, the peer review it carries); absent that evidence, drop a tier — a source you cannot anchor to a concrete basis is weaker than it looks. Corroboration never promotes a source *across* tiers: any number of independent community-anecdotal sources agreeing stays community-anecdotal, below one vetted-secondary source on the same claim ([triangulate-before-trusting](triangulate-before-trusting.md)); corroboration lifts a *claim's confidence*, not a *source's strength*.

## The independence gate — checked at every tier, not just the bottom

Independence is not only the tier-4/5 boundary; it is a **gate applied at every tier as you walk down**, because a vested interest that would shape a claim disqualifies a source from the strong tiers regardless of its form. The gate turns on the *kind of claim*, and one discriminator settles the borderline in each direction:

- **Definitional claims** — what a system is *specified* to do: the rules, parameters, and limits its own contract **binds itself to**. Here the first party is the accountable authority with no incentive to misdescribe its own contract, so its own reference is **primary-authoritative**. The borderline is the quantitative case: a number is *definitional* when the contract **binds** itself to it (a documented, guaranteed rate limit the system is held to) and *evaluative* when the source merely **advertises** it (an unbound "handles up to 10k requests/second" performance figure) — the test is whether the system is held to the number, not whether the number appears in its docs.
- **Evaluative or comparative claims** — is it good, fast, safe, *better than* the alternative. Here a vested interest caps the tier, and the **checkable-basis** discriminator sets where it lands. The object of the check is the *claim*, not the exact run: can you cross-check the claim against independent evidence? A stake-holding source is **expert-attributed** when it discloses enough — a named, independently-published benchmark and comparator, or its method and conditions — to weigh the claim against independent results, even without re-running its exact benchmark; it is **unattributable-or-conflicted** when it offers nothing to anchor a cross-check: a bare figure with no benchmark, comparator, or method named, so the vendor's interest is the claim's only warrant. Both this bullet and the tier-5 definition test the *same* object (cross-checking the claim), so a conflicted source is not routed to two tiers at once. *(Deliberately open: exactly how much partial disclosure suffices between these two anchors — a standardized benchmark named but its run parameters withheld — is left to executor judgment. Pinning it to a required-parameter checklist would be false precision: what lets you cross-check a given claim against independent evidence varies with the claim, and the two anchors plus the cross-check-the-claim test already converge the realistic classes.)*

So the vendor engineer's blog is **primary-authoritative** for "the API accepts these parameters" (definitional), and for "our product is faster than X" (evaluative) it is **expert-attributed** if it names a benchmark and comparator you can weigh against independent results, or **unattributable-or-conflicted** if it only asserts the win with nothing to cross-check. Name which kind of claim you are tiering; the same source earns different tiers for different claims. (basis: the independence / conflict-of-interest axis that CRAAP ("Purpose"), SIFT ("agenda"), WP:INDEPENDENT, and ICD 203 (source motivation/bias) all treat as first-order — a source's stake in the answer is weighed before its form; the checkable-basis line mirrors SIFT's "trace to a method you can re-run" and the reproducibility norm the evidence tiers rest on.)

## The domain-hierarchy fork

Some domains carry their own established, authoritative evidence hierarchy — clinical questions have GRADE and the Oxford CEBM levels; legal questions have a binding precedent hierarchy (constitution → statute → binding precedent → persuasive authority). Inside such a domain, **defer to its hierarchy** — it is more authoritative than this general scale, and its practitioners expect it. Outside one — the open-web questions this skill mostly serves — use the general five-tier scale above. The fork is **non-gating**: name which hierarchy you applied so the reader can check it, and when a question straddles a domain boundary, the domain hierarchy governs the in-domain claims and the general scale the rest. (basis: GRADE/CEBM *are* the ratified clinical hierarchy and the legal source hierarchy is black-letter; deferring to a domain's own authority over a general-purpose scale is the same authority-over-anecdote principle the scale itself rests on.)
