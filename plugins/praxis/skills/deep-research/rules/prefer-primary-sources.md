# Prefer primary sources

A claim is only as trustworthy as the origin it traces to. Most of what the web repeats is derivative — a summary of a summary, each hop quietly dropping a caveat — and trust attaches to the origin, not the restatement. Cited from [gather-evidence](../phases/03-gather-evidence.md) and [verify-claims](../phases/04-verify-claims.md).

1. **Trace before trusting.** For any load-bearing claim, follow the citation chain to the source that first established it — the standard, the paper, the primary data, the maintainer's own docs. A blog post citing a paper is a lead *to the paper*, not evidence for the claim.
2. **A derivative source is a pointer, not proof.** When you can only reach a derivative, cite it as what it is ("X reports that [study] found …") and weigh it at the derivative's strength ([weight-by-source-strength](weight-by-source-strength.md)) until you reach the primary — never launder a secondhand claim up to the authority of the source it cites.
3. **Watch for the broken chain.** When the trail dead-ends before any primary source — everyone cites everyone, no origin — that is not corroboration, it is circular reporting (citogenesis). Treat the claim as unverified and say so ([triangulate-before-trusting](triangulate-before-trusting.md)); apparent agreement among echoes of one origin is a single source, not many.

(basis: the primary/secondary/tertiary source taxonomy and SIFT's fourth move — "trace claims, quotes, and media to the original context" — the primary-vs-derivative axis common to evidence-evaluation frameworks.)
