# Prefer primary sources

Most of what gets repeated is derivative — a summary of a summary, each hop quietly dropping a caveat — and trust attaches to the origin, not the restatement.

1. **Trace before trusting.** For any load-bearing claim, follow the citation chain to the source that first established it — the standard, the paper, the primary data, the maintainer's own documentation. A post citing a paper is a lead *to the paper*, not evidence for the claim.
2. **A derivative source is a pointer, not proof.** When you can only reach a derivative, cite it as what it is ("X reports that [study] found …") and weigh it at the derivative's strength until you reach the primary — never launder a secondhand claim up to the authority of the source it cites.
3. **Watch for the broken chain.** When the trail dead-ends before any primary source — everyone cites everyone, no origin — that isn't corroboration, it's circular reporting (citogenesis). The claim is **single-source**, on the support scale [results-and-certainty](results-and-certainty.md) defines, and you say so; apparent agreement among echoes of one origin is one origin, not many.

The test for whether you've reached the primary: **is this the source that would publish the correction if the claim were wrong?** That is whoever owns the claim — the vendor for its own product's behavior, the standard or the paper for a domain result, the maintainer for their project's contract. A source explaining someone else's claim is a secondary reading of it, however authoritative it is on its own ground.

(basis: the primary/secondary/tertiary source taxonomy; SIFT's fourth move)
