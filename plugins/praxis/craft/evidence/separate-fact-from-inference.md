# Separate fact from inference

A result is trustworthy only when its reader can tell what a source *said*, or what was seen, from what you *concluded* from it. Blur the two and the conclusion silently inherits the authority of its input — the commonest way a result overstates what it actually knows — and the reader goes forward confident about something nobody established.

1. **Keep the two distinct on every finding.** The source's own claim (verbatim where load-bearing, with its anchor) is one thing; the inference you drew from it is another, carrying only the grade your reasoning earns on the scale its claim takes ([results-and-certainty](results-and-certainty.md)) — never the source's standing.
2. **An inference stays at the strength of its weakest input.** A conclusion built from a derivative or weak source is itself weak, however sound the reasoning: strength travels with the source, not with your confidence in the conclusion. Combining two weak findings doesn't manufacture a strong one.
3. **Label the inference as yours in the output.** "The spec says X" and "so this behavior is probably wrong" are different statements, each graded on its own: state them separately, never merged into "the behavior is wrong per the spec." An assumption carried in from the question is an inference too, and owes the same label.

The test for each statement: **would it still stand if you deleted your reasoning and kept only the source?** If yes, it's the source's claim, carried at the source's strength. If it needs your reasoning to stand, it's your inference, labeled as yours and carried at the grade your reasoning earns.

(basis: the anchoring discipline of authoritative sourcing)
