# Design reader instruments

A tab's instrument is the picture its reader works from: the map, the trace, the gate rail. Drawn from the fields alone, an instrument shows everything with equal weight and leaves the reader to find what matters; built to these points, it tells them before they look. (basis: maintainer, 2026-11-12, judging rendered tabs against a hand-iterated reference)

1. **The conclusion comes before the picture.** An instrument opens with one to three headlines computed from the store. The candidates, in this order, are: what nobody owns or decides; what is overdue or blocks a step; where to start on the critical path; what is missing that the tab's type requires. Each that holds is said, up to three; when none holds, one line says so. Each is a claim with its count and the item it names: "Start with UNIT-5: it can start now and 21 things follow from it", never "21 things". A headline names an item a reader can open, and selects it where the picture can. A tab with nothing worth saying states that: "Every gate has a day and a decider".
2. **Attention goes only where someone must act.** Colour, size and labels mark what is open, missing, overdue or on the critical path, and nothing else: red for what needs an action (unowned, missing, failing, one-way), amber for what is unsettled (open, leaning, proposed, not tested), green for what holds (agreed, merged, verified), the accent for the reader's way through. Finished work fades or folds into a summary line; what nobody owns is shown as owned by nobody, first. Size, where a picture has it, follows how many open items wait on an item, directly or not.
3. **One reading order.** A page reads top to bottom: where it stands, who must act, what the parts are, the proof. Never columns of unequal depth side by side, which leave the page blank beside the longest; one full-width list instead, in that order.
4. **A picture where items link, a list where they don't.** A tab draws a picture when the items it shows link to each other or place themselves on an axis (gates, dates, components); otherwise it is one list. **Show the web, give it a spine.** Where items depend on each other, every link stays visible, faint, and the path that decides the outcome is drawn strong. Hiding links to tidy the picture loses what the reader came for; laying items out by depth alone turns a chain into a staircase.
5. **Select to focus,** owed by every picture. Selecting an item brings its own links forward and dims the rest, and opens its story beside the picture: what it is, where it stands, its evidence with each check's outcome, what it waits on and what it unlocks, each a link. A link to a tab and an item opens it selected.
6. **Words from the reader's side.** Items show by their code, which reads compactly, gates and slices by their step code ("C1"); every code names its kind and title on hover, and every page carries a key of the codes the document uses, so a reader never has to remember one. (basis: maintainer, 2026-11-13, codes kept, with a reminder present across the document) Titles are the item's own short title; fields show as what they mean ("Before", "Roll back", "Left behind", "Why this tier") rather than as schema names ("go when", "relative to none"). A missing value is said as missing ("nobody named", "not set", "no target yet"), never left blank or invented.
7. **One family across tabs.** Every tab opens the same way (kicker, headline, badge, lead, then its headlines) and draws state, severity and links with the same marks, one shared stylesheet the renderer applies being where they live, so a reader learns them once.
8. **Every hosted item has a place.** Every item of a type a tab hosts appears on the tab, in its instrument or in a section its design shows; an item only the page as filed shows is a gap, not a choice.

The backend's renderer checks an instrument against the points that can be computed; the rest are judged by reading the rendered page.
