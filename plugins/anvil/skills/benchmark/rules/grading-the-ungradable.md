An A/B comparison is only as trustworthy as the grader that scores the two arms, and a grader is only trustworthy where an *oracle* exists — some fixed answer both arms can be measured against that two graders would read identically. This is the oracle problem, and it is the crux of turning two piles of run logs into a verdict. Some outcomes have an oracle and can be graded cold; some have none and need a judge whose reading is inherently softer; and some have neither, where the honest move is to record that and grade nothing. The whole of this rule is drawing those three lines without flinching — because a confidently wrong grade poisons the comparison worse than an admitted gap. A missing grade only narrows the evidence; a false grade turns the verdict the wrong way with borrowed authority.

## Prefer the contract-gradable slice

The kit already defines what a correct skill is, and it already ships the oracles that check it: the critics. For a whole class of outcomes "better" and "worse" are not matters of taste but of contract conformance, and each such outcome has a critic whose charter is exactly that check. The discriminator for whether an outcome belongs in this slice is precise — an outcome is contract-gradable when a named critic's charter covers it, because that is the test two graders reach identically:

- Did a run stall where a cold executor could not proceed, or would two cold executors diverge on the same step → the [cold-executor](../../../agents/critics/cold-executor.md).
- Did the skill layer name a concrete tool where a capability belongs → the [leak-hunter](../../../agents/critics/leak-hunter.md).
- Did the frontmatter shape, slot placement, or flag↔module wiring break → the [contract-skeptic](../../../agents/critics/contract-skeptic.md).
- Did a bar that grades, ranks, or selects go unpinned with no recorded reason → the [standards-skeptic](../../../agents/critics/standards-skeptic.md).
- Did a phase, rule, module, or flag stop earning its place → the [scaffolding-skeptic](../../../agents/critics/scaffolding-skeptic.md).
- Did a passage stop earning its keep — derivable prose, a re-derivation of a cited file, an instruction with a second home → the [economy-skeptic](../../../agents/critics/economy-skeptic.md).
- Did authoring-only material cross into what ships, or a plugin's ships-vs-authoring line move wrong → the [boundary-keeper](../../../agents/critics/boundary-keeper.md).

Grade this slice by turning those same lenses on each arm's run logs and on the diff between the arms — the critics the kit uses at authoring time, now used comparatively. Because the answer is fixed by the contract, the slice is objective (two graders converge), cheap (the oracles already exist — no bespoke harness to build), and reusable across every benchmark run. Lead with it. A regression here is unarguable: a scenario that ran clean on the baseline arm and stalls, leaks a tool name, or breaks its frontmatter on the changed arm is a regression no reasonable reader disputes — and it is exactly the silent break (a change that fixes scenario A while quietly breaking scenario B) that this skill exists to catch. Prefer the contract slice for every outcome it can reach, and drop to a softer tier only for what it genuinely cannot.

## The subjective residue is the softer signal

Below the contract lies quality no critic covers: is the authored prose genuinely good, is an explanation actually clearer to a reader, does the changed skill simply run more smoothly. No contract pins these, so no critic can grade them; the fallback is a judging pass — a separate, blind reading taken *only* for the residue the critics leave, never as a stand-in for them, and a reading performed under discipline rather than another agent to recruit. Two disciplines make that reading as trustworthy as a soft signal can be, each pinned with the reason for its tightness:

- Run the judge blind to which arm is the baseline and which is the change. Blind, because an unblinded judge anchors on the expectation that the newer arm is the improvement and grades toward it, manufacturing a delta out of novelty bias rather than quality.
- Give the judge one identical rubric and score both arms in a single pass. One rubric, one pass, because two separately-framed gradings drift apart on framing alone, and that drift reads as a quality difference that was never there. The rubric is a forced preference, not a score: holding both arms' outputs side by side, which reads as better on the single residue dimension in question — arm A, arm B, or tie — with a one-line basis. A preference and not a number on purpose: a fabricated 1–5 is exactly the false precision the next section forbids, and a soft signal that never decides a verdict needs only a direction and a reason, not a magnitude.

Even so, this signal is inherently noisier than the contract slice — judge-to-judge and run-to-run variance is larger here, and no amount of blinding removes it entirely. So the pinned bar on its authority: the subjective residue never decides a verdict on its own. It may corroborate a contract-slice result or add color to the report, but a verdict — improvement, regression, within-noise — must rest on the contract slice; a flip that only the judge sees is reported as a soft observation, not a verdict. The reason for that tightness: a noisy signal driving a binary decision converts its own variance into false verdicts at the rate of its noise, and a benchmark that ships false verdicts is worse than no benchmark at all. (How large a judge delta must be before it counts as real at all, and how many repeats hold its variance down, are [signal-vs-noise](signal-vs-noise.md)'s to pin; this rule only bounds what the signal is allowed to decide.)

## Do not manufacture an oracle

The failure that undoes the whole method is inventing precision where none exists — grading an outcome that has no oracle as though it had one. It takes two shapes, and both are worse than an honest gap.

The first is the bespoke per-skill assertion: a hand-written check that passes because it was written against these exact runs, not because it captures anything the contract cares about. It grades the corpus, not the skill — it will wave through a regression that happens to dodge the specific thing you asserted, and fail an improvement that reaches the same outcome by different words. Overfitting the handful of scenarios under test is how a benchmark quietly comes to measure its own test data instead of the change; prefer the reusable critic, and treat every bespoke assertion as a debt to justify against [scenario-corpus-discipline](scenario-corpus-discipline.md).

The second is the false-precision metric: attaching a number to an outcome that is neither contract-gradable nor meaningfully judgeable, because a number reads as rigor. It is worse than silence, because the fabricated figure does not stay a harmless curiosity — it flows into the aggregation and moves the verdict with an authority it never earned, and no one downstream can tell it from a grade that was actually measured.

So the rule, stated plainly: if an outcome is neither contract-gradable nor meaningfully judgeable, record it as *not measurable on this corpus* and exclude it from the decision — say so in the report rather than invent a figure. An honest "not measurable here" costs the comparison a single data point; a fabricated grade costs it its trustworthiness, which is the only thing a benchmark has to sell. And when it is unclear which slice an outcome belongs to, drop it a tier rather than raise it — grade it softly, or not at all, before you lend the contract slice's authority to something the contract never covered.
