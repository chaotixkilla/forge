When phase 02 fans out for the real shape of a process, it comes back with a pile of signal of wildly uneven quality — a standard here, a reference doc there, a handful of forum threads, one strongly-worded blog post. The failure is to treat them as equal and average them into mush, which buries the authoritative answer under volume and lets the loudest anecdote win. The craft is to **turn every stone but weight the signal**: gather widely, then judge what you gathered by where it came from and how independently it's confirmed.

Turn every stone first — breadth is cheap and a process's canonical shape often hides in a source you didn't expect to consult. But gathering is not believing. Once the findings are in, rank them. **Authoritative sources outrank anecdote**: an official standard, a reference specification, a published paper, a canonical book carries more weight than a single one-off community discussion. The authority isn't about prestige — it's about whether the source is *accountable for being right*, which a standard is and a passing comment isn't.

One source is easy to misplace on that ladder: yourself. When the signal being weighed is a **standard's bar** — a threshold the procedure will enforce, a grade boundary, what its output must count as "good" — the model's own default answer sits at the anecdote tier, not above it. A default for a bar is the average of common practice, and a bar that restates average practice encodes nothing — average is what a stated bar exists to improve on. So a bar is never default-filled. The obligation is **derive and propose**, and it lands as an inline marker written onto the pinned bar: pull a candidate from an accountable source and write the bar carrying **(basis: <the source or derivation>)**; where no accountable source reaches the call, still pin your best defensible candidate but write it carrying **(routed to maintainer: <the candidate + why>)** so the maintainer ratifies rather than excavates. A pinned bar written with neither marker is the default-fill this obligation forbids — a fiat assertion, not a closed bar.

**Corroboration across independent sources boosts signal.** Several sources agreeing is strong evidence something real is there — but only if they're genuinely independent. Ten posts all quoting the same original aren't ten signals; they're one signal echoed ten times, and counting the echoes inflates a weak claim into a false consensus. Trace agreement back to its origins before you treat it as corroboration.

**Conflict is an attrition point, not a coin flip.** When the anecdote says X and the authority says not-X, two things must happen, both of them: favor the authoritative source in what you encode, *and* surface the conflict to the maintainer rather than resolving it silently. The reason for the second half is that an authority can be stale or wrong for *this* team's context, and the maintainer is the one who knows — so the conflict is a decision to hand up, not a problem to bury. A silently-resolved conflict becomes a latent bug no one knew was a choice; a surfaced one is a decision made by the right owner with the disagreement in full view.

**A fork between authorities is not a conflict to win.** When two accountable sources genuinely disagree — each defensible in context — picking a winner is as much a defect as burying the conflict: you'd be settling a contested question with less standing than either source. Encode the fork instead: name each position and its tradeoff in one line, then give the routing rule — the established convention of the surrounding context wins if one exists, else a declared house rule, else the maintainer — and mark it non-gating, so the run proceeds whichever way it routes rather than stalling on the disagreement.

**Complementary options are not a fork — don't manufacture one.** A subtler case sits right next to the genuine fork, and it recurs: several accountable sources each endorse a *different* option, but the sources frame them as **complementary and differently-scoped**, not as rivals — each fits a different situation, and choosing one doesn't reject the others. Two wrong encodings tempt here. Forcing it into an either/or fork invents a controversy the authorities don't claim ("A vs B" where the sources say "A for this, B for that"). Treating "no genuine conflict" as licence to just pick one and drop the rest loses the routing exactly as a silent conflict-resolution does. Encode it as its own shape: a **sourced default** (the option the authorities treat as the baseline), a one-line **"reach for each other option when …"** naming the situation each fits, and the same **routing rule** when the surrounding context or a house rule already settled it — and say outright that the options are complementary, so a reader doesn't infer a false conflict. The discriminator against a genuine fork: in a fork the positions *compete* — each pays a cost the other avoids, so you can't hold both; in a complementary set they *coexist* — they cover different scopes, so the question is which fits *here*, not which wins. Encode this as a first-class sibling of the genuine-conflict fork, not an afterthought.

The anti-pattern is the confident silent resolution — picking a side in a conflict and writing it in as settled fact, with no trace that the other side existed. It produces a procedure that looks authoritative and is quietly contested, and the next maintainer has no way to know a judgment call was even made. Weight the signal, favor authority over anecdote, encode the fork when authorities disagree — and either way, let the maintainer see the fight.
