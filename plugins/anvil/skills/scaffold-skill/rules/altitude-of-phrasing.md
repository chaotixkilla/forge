The skill layer names capabilities; tools live only in adapters. That's the hard rule the whole kit is built to defend, and the cheapest place to honor it is at authoring time — in the words you choose for a phase, a description, a rule. Every concrete tool name you don't write into the skill layer is a leak the audit never has to find and a maintainer never has to relocate. So this rule is the discipline applied while writing: phrase at the altitude of *what is accomplished*, never *which tool accomplishes it*. There are two independent dimensions to get right — the *altitude* (capability vs. tool) and the *granularity* (step vs. sentence vs. sub-project) — and they're easy to conflate, so treat them separately.

## Altitude — name the capability, never the tool

Write what the step *does*, independent of any backend: *"publish the report to the configured artifacts backend"*, not the name of the product or page where it lands; *"open a change request"*, not the CLI or service that opens it. The reliable test is the swap test: if the configured provider changed tomorrow, would this sentence still be true and unchanged? If yes, you've named a capability; if the sentence would have to be rewritten, you named a tool. A phase that survives a provider swap is a phase at the right altitude.

A subtler failure than naming a tool is *inventing a mechanism*. When you gesture at how a capability connects to its implementation, describe the *relationship* in terms the kit has actually fixed — a phase names a capability, a dispatch routes that capability to whichever adapter is configured, a flag activates a module — and stop there. Do not write a literal dispatch syntax, a manifest schema, or a config key shape as if it were settled fact: those are mechanisms the kit hasn't frozen, and baking an invented one into a skill is its own kind of leak (a fact that will go stale or contradict the real mechanism when it lands). Name the relationship using established convention; never show fabricated syntax. A clean capability example reads *"the phase invokes the artifacts capability; the dispatch resolves it to the configured adapter"* — it illustrates the seam without pinning a mechanism or a vendor.

## Granularity — a phase is a step

The second dimension is size, and it's orthogonal to altitude: a phase can be perfectly tool-free and still be the wrong size. A phase is *a step* — a unit of work with a purpose and a done-state. Too fine and it's a single sentence that should have been a line inside a neighboring phase; the procedure fragments into a dozen micro-phases nobody can hold in their head. Too coarse and it's a sub-project — "do the whole release" — that hides three real steps inside one and gives a cold executor no checkpoints. The calibration: a phase should have enough substance to open with a *why* and close with a *done*, but not so much that it needs sub-headings for distinct sub-procedures (those sub-procedures are the real phases). When a phase wants an "and" in its own name, it's two phases — the same conjunction smell that splits skills splits phases. Whether a step of this size earns its own phase *file* at all is a different question — that bar is the depth test in [choosing-slots](choosing-slots.md).

## Borrow the plugin's voice

Altitude and granularity have a right band, but within that band a plugin has a house style — its characteristic verbs, its density, how much *why* it fronts before the *how*. Recruit the plugin explorer to read the target plugin's existing phases and match it: a new skill should read as a native of its plugin, not a generic template dropped in. If the plugin's phases are terse and imperative, match that; if they open with a paragraph of rationale, match that. Mirroring the local voice is not cosmetic — consistency is what lets a maintainer move between a plugin's skills without re-learning how to read each one. But its scope is voice only — verbs, density, how much *why* fronts the *how* — never the contract: which slots exist, which fields are declared, and what the seed calls for come from the interrogated need and the documented contract, and a sibling that predates those conventions is a backlog to fix, not prior art to copy. Where the plugin is brand-new with no siblings, fall back to the kit's register (developed but tight: the why, the detailed how, capability-level examples, anti-patterns), and let the plugin's own voice accrete from there.

## When a step can only be said by naming a tool

Sometimes a step genuinely seems to *require* a tool name — there's a specific call, a specific parameter, a specific quirk of one provider, and you can't write the step honestly without it. That feeling is the signal, and the answer is structural, not verbal: the tool-intrinsic detail belongs in an *adapter*, and the phase names the capability that dispatches to it. The phase says *"open the change request via the configured provider"*; the adapter holds the actual call and its quirks. You are not losing the detail — you're putting it where it's allowed to live and where a provider swap can replace it without touching the phase. If you find yourself fighting to keep a tool name out of a phase, stop fighting the words and move the detail down a layer; the phrasing problem dissolves once the detail is in the right place.

## The cost of getting it wrong

A tool name in the skill layer is caught downstream by the tool-leak audit and its leak-hunter critic, which will report it with a location and demand a capability rephrasing or a relocation to an adapter. An invented mechanism stated as fact is worse — it isn't a vendor leak the audit pattern-matches, it's a stale-fact landmine that a future maintainer believes until it bites. Both are cheaper to never write than to find and fix. This rule is the authoring-time discipline that keeps the audit's job to confirming, not repairing.
